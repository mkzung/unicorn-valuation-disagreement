*Solo working paper, public data only. Max Gorbuk, Independent Researcher, MAM, London Business School.*
*Paper: [`paper/unicorn_valuation_disagreement.pdf`](paper/unicorn_valuation_disagreement.pdf) · [SSRN abstract 7016178](https://ssrn.com/abstract=7016178). Reproduce everything: `pip install -r requirements.lock && python3 src/reproduce.py` (Python ≥ 3.10; the offline pipeline needs no network, and takes about five minutes — `--only stage,stage` runs a subset where a shell caps command time). **Resources, measured rather than assumed:** the canonical-number check alone peaks at ~1.0 GB of RSS and takes ~3 minutes on one core; the full `pytest` suite takes ~5½ minutes; `data/` is 24 MB and the parquet panel cache adds ~19 MB. ## The claim

A private company has no *continuously observable* price. It has a price at a transaction — a round, a tender, a secondary block — and between transactions its value is whatever its holders say it is. This paper observes what every US registered fund said, on the population rather than a sample, and measures how far apart they are.

**The headline.** Holding the company and the report date fixed, independent fund **houses** mark the same private share a median of **12.1%** apart (**10.1%** across the venture-backed names); only 17.0% of company-dates are unanimous; **$180.0B** of the **$517.3B** booked in these positions sits above a 24% spread. The unit matters more than any other choice in the paper: N-PORT names a legal trust, a house files under dozens of them, and measuring between registrants gives **0.004%** — agreement manufactured by counting one valuation committee thirty times.

**Four falsification tests it survives, and the fourth bounds it.** On Level-1 holdings the same two houses agree to the cent (§2.3). On 760 cells where *no* house moved its mark, two thirds are not unanimous (§6.2). On one lot where the entry price is disclosed and provably identical — Databricks Series J at **$92.50** a share, carried by two houses to **$190.00** over 2025 and printed by four filings on four cost bases as the identical ratio 76/37 — the two are **eleven dollars a share apart** at the midpoint and identical to nine decimals at the end (§2.1); that lot is one of four disagreements among 45 comparable lots, so it establishes existence and not frequency. The fourth runs on the population and constrains the headline: where the filings name the series and two houses name the **same** one, the median gap is **0.74%** against **8.45%** on those same cells scored the usual way, while the share above 24% falls only from 35.4% to 22.0% — **597 groups across 68 companies where two houses hold the same named series and differ by more than a quarter** (§3.3, Appendix C.5). Composition explains the typical gap between two houses; it does not explain the wide one. The same appendix states what the test cannot do: the cells no filing describes are the widest group in the panel (16.42%) and the most numerous (1,941 of 4,271), so composition is unmeasurable exactly where disagreement is largest.

**What compresses it.** A transaction, and only briefly. Rounds are dated from the filings themselves (§9); disagreement falls **2.52 points** across the round month, is narrower afterwards in 22 of 29 companies, repeats at a company's own later rounds, and rebuilds at about **1.1 points a month**. All three shifted anchors show no step at all. The result is bounded and the bound is printed beside it: 49 dated anchors, a magnitude resting on a dating bar that discards most of the panel's series letters, and seven down rounds — too few to separate "a price now exists" from "the news was good" (§8.4).

**What adjudicates it.** §7 scores the headline against the last pre-IPO N-PORT mark across ten 2023–26 listings; the fund mark is least-wrong in **five of** the seven where mutual funds held the company pre-IPO (Instacart +8% vs +294%; Chime −1% vs +116%; Reddit −5% vs +56%; ServiceTitan +11% vs +21%; Figma −28% vs −48% floor), median |error| **11% vs 48%**. The **two counter-examples (Klaviyo, Circle)** are exactly the recent, fairly-priced rounds, so the headline was not stale — which sharpens the claim to "the fund mark beats the headline *when, and only when, the headline has gone stale*". The marks are not prescient; they are simply not stale.

Anchor: **Gornall & Strebulaev (2020)** priced *deal terms* and found headline valuations ~48% above fair value. This paper's object is the **dispersion** of what sophisticated holders report rather than the level of the headline, on the population rather than a sample; Appendix C.1 reconciles the two.

## Four instruments recovered from filings (§9)

Each is calibrated against an independent document type, and each is usable without this paper's data:

1. **Listing dates** from a company's own Form 8-A12B / 8-K12B — 21 of 23 names, validated against the last date the panel carries them private (18 inside a quarter, widest 82 days against a next-closest 258).
2. **Share splits** off the share-count panel — 601 candidates, **29 events** confirmed by two or more houses. Its own finding: restatement is *not* simultaneous (median span 30 days, up to 92), which is why a one-month confirmation window is wrong and why the price-coordination dating rule below fails.
3. **Round dates** from the first month a new series letter appears across two houses — **434** company-series pairs on **287** companies, calibrated against N-CSR acquisition dates to within 35 days on **14 of 15** clean pairs (median gap 16 days).
4. **Acquisition dates and cost** from N-CSR schedules of investments — 767 schedule rows, 44 registrants, 429 lot-period-series, prefilter validated as a superset with **zero missed**.

A negative result is reported with them: **price coordination dates worse than the count rule** (11 of 15 inside 35 days against 14 of 15, at a median gap of 20 days against 16; the step's magnitude goes with the anchor and its sign does not), because waiting for a second house to agree waits on that house's reporting calendar rather than on the company's — the desynchronisation instrument 2 measures.

## Data sources

- **N-PORT (fund marks), by company.** EDGAR full-text search (`efts.sec.gov/LATEST/search-index?q="<company>"&forms=NPORT-P`) → resolve filing → parse the `invstOrSec` private holdings (issuer, fair value, level) from the NPORT-P XML; `src/nport_fetch.py` and `src/nport_timeseries.py` (a real SEC `User-Agent` header is required).
- **N-PORT, by filing.** The SEC's quarterly [Form N-PORT data sets](https://www.sec.gov/data-research/sec-markets-data/form-n-port-data-sets) hold every disseminated filing from 2019Q4 onward. `src/nport_bulk.py` harvests them into `data/nport_population_marks.csv.gz`, which finds companies instead of assuming them: the search above can only reach names already on a list and stops after eighteen filings each. `src/entity_resolution.py` maps issuer strings to companies and is held to the hand-labelled companies in `data/fund_marks.csv` by `tests/test_entity_resolution.py`; `src/population.py` builds the company-by-report-date panel. `notes/universe_definition.md` sets out which rows qualify and why the restricted-security flag is not one of the conditions. Which figures come from which path: every number in the PDF comes from the by-company harvest, and `src/reconcile_versions.py` recomputes each of those §4.3 cells from the by-filing panel so the two can be compared cell by cell.
- **Headline rounds.** Last primary-round valuations from company announcements and the financial press, each independently ≥2-sourced.
- **Cut to a second paper.** A secondary-market cross-section, a vendor private-market index and exchange-traded IPO contracts were reported in an earlier version. They rest on sources outside EDGAR, one takes the primary round as a modelling input, and no claim here needs them. Their code and data stay in this repository; their six figures do not, because `figures/` is what a reader browses and a chart of a signal the paper says it does not use is a chart that will be read as one. `tests/test_manuscript_pointers.py` holds `figures/` to exactly the images the paper cites.

## Results at a glance

Every result below is reproducible and robustness-checked. **One command reproduces everything:** `pip install -r requirements.lock` then `python3 src/reproduce.py` runs every offline stage in order (`validation`, `fund_marks`, `robustness`, `level1_placebo`, `mark_staleness`, `fund_complex`, `population`, `population_figure`, `fund_marks_bulk`, `company_class`, `p4_pretest`, `reconcile_versions`, `split_events`, `round_dates`, `round_event_study`, `nav_wedge`, `paper_figures`), regenerates the figures, and recomputes every headline number quoted in the paper from the production code — checking each against the manuscript prose and writing `notes/reproduction_manifest.md`. The registry of canonical figures lives in `src/paper_numbers.py`; `tests/test_paper_consistency.py` turns any code- *or* prose-drift into a CI failure (the failure mode that previously had to be caught by hand). Ten scripts reach out to SEC and are run separately: `src/family_forecast.py`, `src/form_d.py`, `src/listing_dates.py`, `src/ncen_advisers.py`, `src/ncsr_acquisitions.py`, `src/nport_bulk.py`, `src/nport_fetch.py`, `src/nport_timeseries.py`, `src/verify_marks.py`, `src/verify_placebo_sec.py`. They refresh or verify the data the offline pipeline then reads, and they overwrite committed files in place — EDGAR full-text search does not return the same set twice, so read `git diff` after a refresh before keeping it.

1. **IPO-exit validation — which pre-IPO signal was least wrong** (10 exits, 7 fund-held, `src/validation.py`): two pre-IPO signals scored against the realized IPO. The four 2021-vintage down-round **headlines** overshot the IPO by a median **+160%**, and the sign is demand-conditional rather than cycle-timed: repriced consumer-fintech (Chime +116%, Klarna +205% — both HEADLINE errors; Klarna has no broad fund mark and its interim signal is a 2022 down round) overshot while a compounder (Figma −48% floor) and a momentum name (SpaceX/xAI −29%) listed *above* their last round. Across the **seven** exits where mutual funds held the company pre-IPO, the last **SEC N-PORT mark** was least-wrong in **five**: **Instacart +8% vs +294%** (35× closer; T. Rowe ×4 + American Funds $32.50/sh ⇒ ≈$10.7B vs $9.9B / $30 IPO), **Chime −1% vs +116%** (4 Alger funds $26.77/sh, within ~1% of the $27 IPO six weeks pre-listing), **Reddit −5% vs +56%** (Fidelity ×3 $32.37 ⇒ ≈$6.1B vs $6.4B / $34), **ServiceTitan +11% vs +21%** (T. Rowe ×4 $78.85/sh, structured Series H headline), and even Figma's floor case **−28% vs −48%** (Fidelity OTC ×2 $23.73/sh). Median |fund-mark error| **11%** vs |headline| **48%**. The **two counter-examples are the tell** — Klaviyo (headline +3% beats the mark's +15%) and Circle (headline +11% beats Fidelity's conservative −25%, which *undershot* a +168% pop) are exactly the names whose last round was recent and fairly priced, so the headline was not stale: the fund mark's edge **is the absence of staleness, not foresight**. Klarna's 2022 down round overcorrected (**−56%**) yet still beat its +205% headline. So the very marks that *disagree* in §4.3 carry better exit information than the headline, and only where the headline has gone stale. `figures/ipo_validation.png`.
2. **N-PORT cross-fund marks** (386 Level-3 marks, 104 mutual funds, 15 companies, from SEC EDGAR): holding the report date fixed, funds disagree on the per-share value of the *same* private security by a median **24%** (10 names with ≥5 same-date funds), up to **+39% Anthropic** (14 funds, identical security $259–$361), **+35% Revolut** (ARK Venture ~26% below the Fidelity/T. Rowe field) and **+32% Gusto** (3 families), with **+15% Databricks** (12 funds) — yet **herd** to one price for fresh-round names (**OpenAI 13 funds all $687.69**, Stripe +1%, Anduril +4%); disagreement clusters by **fund family**. Excluded as non-comparable composites: SpaceX (multi-class) and **ByteDance** (Douyin Co Ltd vs ByteDance Ltd entities + common vs convertible-preferred classes). Widening the panel from 8 to 10 broadly-held names roughly **doubled the median (13%→24%)** — the added names sit in the high-disagreement mode; the bimodality (herd vs disagree) is the robust feature. `figures/fund_marks_dispersion.png`.



3. **Robustness** (`src/robustness.py` → `data/robustness_summary.csv`, Appendix B): every headline result survives the obvious attacks. The 24% cross-fund spread (10 names with ≥5 funds) is invariant to the unit-outlier band (23.7% at every K∈[2,5]) and to the fund threshold. A fund-family variance decomposition turns §4.3's qualitative "clusters by family" into a number: among names with material disagreement ≈100% of cross-fund mark variance is *between* families (median η²≈1.00), and within-family spread is exactly zero in 89% of family-cells, so the effective number of independent views is the number of families rather than of funds. The disagreement is also not lag: every family refreshes its mark in most quarters (79% overall, from 58% at the least active house to 92% at the most), and restricting to company-quarters in which *every* family remarked leaves the cross-family spread wider (12.1% against 6.7%), with the same answer across remark tolerances, class guards and a per-company collapse (`src/mark_staleness.py` → `data/mark_staleness.csv`). One rival reading survives and Appendix B says so: conditioning on "every family moved" selects quarters carrying news, so staleness is ruled out without establishing that the difference is permanent policy rather than a repricing that closes in between; §6.2 answers that on the 760 population cells where nobody moved. The structure replicates out of panel: a five-name expansion probe (`data/nport_expansion_probe.csv`) surfaced exactly one further name clearing the ≥5-same-date-funds bar, Fanatics, with 7 funds across three families and the same anatomy (cross-family spread 75%, within-family identical to the cent).


4. **Reconciliation with the anchor paper** (Appendix C.1, no new data): the paper is framed as an out-of-sample update to **Gornall–Strebulaev (2020)** and has to say how its object differs from theirs. G-S price *deal terms* and find reported post-money averaging **48% above** the option-adjusted fair value of the cap table, with common shares **56% overvalued** and 65 of 135 companies losing unicorn status. This paper never re-derives that number and could not: it would need each company's per-round legal terms, which public filings do not carry. What Appendix C.1 establishes is that the two do not conflict and where they meet. G-S's wedge is a statement about the whole cap table; a fund mark is a price for one share class, carried to a company figure by the same single-price-times-shares convention the headline uses, so a headline can be 48% above fair value and still be the number two houses are arguing about. And the prediction is shared: the G-S haircut is largest where the downside protections are deepest in the money, on distressed and repriced names, and collapses toward zero for winners far above the preference stack — which is where §5.2 and §8 find the fund houses furthest apart and closest together respectively. Interpretive, no re-estimation of the option model, and no registered figure moves.
5. **The population panel** (`src/population.py`, `src/entity_resolution.py`, `src/fund_complex.py` → `data/nport_population_marks.csv.gz`, §5): the same cross-fund test with the name list thrown away. Every Level-3 private equity position in all 27 quarterly SEC bulk N-PORT data sets, 2019Q4–2026Q2 — **309,654** marks, **15,443** issuer strings, 104 report dates — resolved to companies by identifier and exact name only, never by a similarity score. Families are fund **complexes**, not registrants: a registrant is one legal trust, and Fidelity files under 36 of them, so counting registrants both lowers the independence bar and books one valuation committee as two agreeing houses (`src/fund_complex.py` covers 98% of booked NAV and fails closed). That map is mine, and item 6 checks it against a source it was not built from. Claim instruments — contingent value rights, escrow lines, subscription rights, warrants, lock-up placeholders — are excluded, because an issuer identifier joins them to the company's stock while their price per unit has nothing to do with it. **4,271** cells across **656** companies clear the ≥5-fund/≥2-complex bar (`src/population_figure.py` → `figures/population_spread.png`). Between independent houses disagreement is the normal state: only **17.0%** of company-dates are unanimous and **28.4%** agree to within a basis point, the median spread is **12.1%**, **40.2%** of cells exceed 24%, and §4.3's ten-name median of 24% sits at the **60th percentile** — scored the population's own way (between house medians, not between funds) §4.3's ten names give 23.7% and land at the same **60th percentile**. **$180.0B** of the **$517.3B** booked in these positions — **34.8%** — sits above 24%. Disagreement is a company trait, not a date effect (between-company variance share **58.8%** vs a **9.7%** relabelling null; lag-1 ρ=**0.734**, and **0.665** restricted to pairs where the top mark moved), it does not dissolve when both houses stop repricing (of 760 cells in which no house moved its mark, 66.4% are not unanimous and 23.6% exceed 24%), and the house effect scales (**89.0%** of **9,210** multi-fund house-cells file one identical mark). Every cluster is then labelled by what kind of company it is (`src/company_class.py` → `data/company_classification.csv`), because the filing system carries listed issuers, buyout portfolio companies and corporate structured preferred alongside the venture-backed names, and only the last are what this paper is about — labels for 118 clusters covering 93.6% of booked value are verified one at a time, the rest by a rule that fires only where the filings are unambiguous and is right **94%** of the time where a verified label exists to check it against. On the venture-backed population alone — **137** distinct issuers, **2,113** cells, $402.2B — the median spread is **10.1%**, **37.0%** of company-dates exceed 24%, and **$152.8B** sits above that line, a *higher* share of the value than on the mixed panel. `src/reconcile_versions.py` recomputes all ten published §4.3 cells from this panel — nine come back with more funds, one matches, none narrower.

6. **The house map, checked from outside** (`src/ncen_advisers.py` → `data/ncen_advisers.csv`, §4): the registrant-to-complex map above is mine, so the correction it produces has to be testable against something else. Item C.9 of Form N-CEN is the SEC's own record of who advises each series. An adviser is recovered for **1,161** of the **1,166** registrants in the panel. Of the **55** houses the map merges, **22** file more than one adviser name, and all 22 are read one by one in the module: 13 are one firm's several advisory entities, 8 are firms it bought that still file their own name, and 1 is an outside manager of a delegated sleeve. None fuses two unrelated firms. The error the filings do show runs the other way, and it is the harmless one: **96** advisers appear under more than one house, each a merge the map declines to make, so each can only understate the correction. The largest is the merge the paper withholds on purpose, and N-CEN confirms it independently: Franklin Advisers now advises nine Putnam trusts. `notes/ncen_validation.md` records the design, and the seven ways this module returned a clean answer while it was broken.

Remaining (optional): the 1,941 cells no filing describes — the widest group in the panel and the one place a real answer would move the headline; and the flow test of Appendix G.4, which needs one more table from the same SEC archives. Core result stands on public data alone; on SSRN (FEN/ERN) at https://ssrn.com/abstract=7016178 — arXiv q-fin.GN next.

## Caveats (stated in full in the paper, §10)

Nothing here is pre-registered, and §10.1 says so first. Fund marks are stale or model-based Level-3 rather than transaction prices, which is the object rather than a defect: the dispersion is what is being measured. The by-company harvest reaches only large, fund-held names and is a lower bound on every spread it reports; the population panel avoids that selection and carries limits of its own. Appendix A.3 and A.4 state both. Identity is resolved on identifiers and exact names, never on a similarity score — an earlier version of this README listed "fuzzy name-matching" as a caveat, which had stopped being true and contradicted §3.2.
